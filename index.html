Research Objective

My research looks to understand the fundamental properties associated with flame propagation at conditions which mimic those of an internal combustion engines through utilizing measurements and direct numerical simulations. My Ph.D. work seeks to contibute high-quality measurement data of zero-dimensional quantities, aka fundamental flame properties, associated with the propagation of a given fuel at known thermodynamic conditions (p, T, X). With the rapid increase in computational and modeling capabilities, physically represetative measurements will be of utmost importance for the combustion community as this data will play a key role in validating predictive models and guide the development of the next generation of internal combustion engines.

My research also looks to extend the techniques currently used to measure laminar flame speeds to taking measurements of highly unstable flame speeds in facilities without optical access. The development of these techniques extends the range of pressures and temperatures at which unstable flame speeds can be safely measured to thermodynamic conditions relevant to internal combustion engines where the flames are always highly unstable. This work will provide experimental data which can be used to validate large scale turbulent flame simulations with implications ranging from high speed propulsion systems to understanding the behavior of a thermonuculear flames which transition to detonation fronts during a Type IA supernova.
